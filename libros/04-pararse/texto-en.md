## Experiment: Does Everything Fall the Same? (pages 22–23)

Do this with a grown-up.

**You need:** a ball, a rolled-up sock, a soft block or toy, and two identical sheets of paper.

1. Stand up and stretch out your arm. Drop each thing from the same height. Which way does it fall? Write it down.
2. Take the two sheets of paper. Crumple one into a little ball and leave the other one flat.
3. Before you try, guess: if you drop them together, which one will hit the floor first?
4. Drop them together from the same height and watch closely.
5. Body challenge: drop the ball from your knee, from your waist, and from above your head. From where does it take longest to reach the floor?

For the paper, write down which one landed first. For the challenge, write where it took longest.

**What we found out:** everything we drop falls toward the floor: gravity pulls it down. The flat sheet falls more slowly than the crumpled one, even though they weigh the same: the air slows it down. And from higher up, the ball takes longer to land.

## For grown-ups (page 24)

**The idea.** Gravity is the force with which the Earth pulls everything toward it. That's why things fall when we let go, and why we stay on the ground. Without air, everything would fall equally fast, no matter how heavy; at home, air slows down light, wide things more, like a flat sheet of paper or a big, soft ball. Standing up means learning to hold the body up against that force: when the legs get tired, gravity wins and the baby plops down.

**The milestone.** Many babies start pulling up to stand while holding on to furniture somewhere between 9 and 12 months. At first they stand up and don't know how to get back down; then they "cruise," taking little sideways steps along the furniture, and a bit later they stand for a few seconds without support. Every baby has their own pace; if you have any concerns, check with your pediatrician.

**Safety.** Standing brings new things within reach: anchor to the wall any furniture that could tip over, remove tablecloths they could pull on, and keep heavy or hot things away from edges. Plopping down on their bottom is normal; it's best to practice barefoot, on a rug, with a grown-up close by.

**Talk about it after reading**

- What things in your home reach higher than your head? And which are lower?
- If you drop a feather and a ball, which one lands first? Why?
- Can you think of something that, when you let go, doesn't fall to the floor right away? (Hint: a helium balloon, a kite.)

## Notas de traducción

- "¡PUM! Me siento de cola" → "BUMP! I plop down on my bottom": "plop down" es la expresión natural para cómo se sienta un bebé de golpe.
- "Pasitos de costado" → "little steps sideways"; en la página para adultos se usa "cruise", el término que usan los pediatras en Estados Unidos.
- Las palabras grandes ABAJO y GRAVEDAD pasan a DOWN y GRAVITY.
