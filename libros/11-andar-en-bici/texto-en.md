## Experiment: How Far Does One Turn Go? (pages 22–23)

**You need:** two round lids of different sizes (from a big jar and a small one), a sticker or colored tape, a pencil, and masking tape.

1. Stick the sticker on the edge of the big lid. Stand it on its edge on the floor, with the sticker touching the floor, and mark that spot with tape.
2. Roll the lid slowly until the sticker touches the floor again: that's one turn! Mark that spot.
3. Before you go on, guess: with the small lid, will one turn go farther or less far?
4. Try it and measure both with your hands: how many hands did each one go?
5. **Challenge:** give the big lid a little push so it rolls on its edge. Does it stay up while it rolls? And when it slows down?

| What I test | I think… | What happened |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Big lid: one turn, how many hands? |  |  |
| Small lid: one turn, how many hands? |  |  |
| The lid rolling: does it fall? |  |  |
| The lid standing still on its edge: does it fall? |  |  |

For the challenge, write down what happens when the lid slows down.

**What we discovered:** With every turn, a wheel moves forward as far as its edge measures: that's why the big lid goes farther in one turn than the small one. And while it rolls, the lid stays up; when it slows down, it falls over, just like the bike.

## For grown-ups (page 24)

**The idea.** With every turn, a wheel moves forward by the length of its edge (its circumference): that's why a big wheel goes farther per turn than a small one. A bike standing still falls over because its two wheels rest on a thin line; when it moves, the rider keeps correcting the handlebars, almost without noticing, and the bike "steers back under" the body. That's why it's easier to balance while moving than standing still. It isn't the wheels that hold you up: it's the motion and all those tiny corrections.

**Turns and cycles.** A cycle is something that repeats and comes back to the start: the pedal going up, forward, down, and back; a lap around the park; the days of the week. Counting turns is one of the first ways of measuring.

**The milestone.** Many children learn to ride a bike without training wheels somewhere between 5 and 6 years old; balance bikes (with no pedals) can help it happen sooner. Every child has their own pace; if you have any concerns, check with your pediatrician.

**Safety.** Always wear a well-fitted helmet, even for a quick ride. Ride in parks, playgrounds, or sidewalks with no traffic, never in the street, and with a grown-up close by. Wear closed-toe shoes, and choose a bike that fits: feet flat on the ground while sitting on the seat.

**Things to talk about after reading:** What other things have wheels? How many wheels does each one have? Why is it easier not to fall when you're moving than when the bike is still? What things repeat every day, like a cycle?
