## Experiment: Where Does the Ball Roll Farther? (pages 22–23)

Do this with a grown-up.

**You need:** a medium-size ball (not a small one), a big hardcover book, two or three more books, and a towel.

1. Build a ramp on the floor: rest one end of the big book on top of the other books.
2. Place the ball at the top of the ramp and let go. Don't push!
3. Measure with your hands how far it went: one hand, two hands, three hands… Write it down.
4. Now spread the towel on the floor at the bottom of the ramp. Let the ball go again, measure, and write it down.
5. Challenge: crawl slowly on a smooth floor and on a rug. Where do your knees slip more?

**What we found out:** on the smooth floor, the ball goes far. On the towel it stops sooner, because the towel slows it down more. The same friction that slows the ball is what keeps your hands and knees from slipping when you crawl.

## For grown-ups (page 24)

**The idea.** Friction appears when two surfaces touch and one moves, or tries to move, across the other. It doesn't only slow things down: it's also what lets us grip and push. When a baby crawls, they push the floor backward with their hands and knees, and friction moves them forward; on a very slippery floor, they slide. Something similar happens with the ball: when it rolls on a rug, the rug sinks in a little and steals its energy, so it stops sooner (physicists call this rolling resistance).

**The milestone.** Many babies start crawling somewhere between 7 and 10 months. Before that, they often rock on their hands and knees, and it's common to go backward at first. Some babies scoot on their bellies, others shuffle on their bottoms, and some hardly crawl at all and go straight to standing. Every baby has their own pace; if you have any concerns, check with your pediatrician.

**Safety.** Once a baby starts moving, the house changes: it helps to check the floor from their eye level. Keep small objects out of reach (if it fits through a toilet paper roll, it's a choking hazard), cover outlets, keep cords out of reach, anchor furniture to the wall, and use a safety gate at the stairs. On very smooth floors, bare feet or non-slip socks are best. And always, a grown-up close by.

**Talk about it after reading**

- Which shoes slip less: the ones with smooth soles or the ones with bumpy patterns underneath?
- Where do you slip more: on a wet floor or a dry one?
- How many legs does a dog use to walk? And how many did you use to crawl?

## Notas de traducción

- "¡FIUUU!" → "WHOOSH!"; la palabra grande FRICCIÓN → FRICTION.
- "Tiene muchos pelitos" → "It has lots of fuzzy little threads": "hairs" en una alfombra suena raro en inglés.
- "Se arrastran, otros se desplazan sentados" → "scoot on their bellies… shuffle on their bottoms", que son los términos que usan los pediatras en Estados Unidos.
