## Experiment: What Takes More Effort? (pages 22–23)

**You need:** a cloth bag with handles, three books, and socks in two colors.

1. Put one book in the bag. Lift it from the floor up to your knee, and then up to your tummy.
2. Before you go on, guess: with three books, will it take more effort or less?
3. Put in all three books and lift it again to both heights.
4. Write down how hard it was each time: a little, more, or a lot.
5. **Challenge:** with a grown-up, make a row of socks on the floor: white, colored, white, colored… Which one comes next?

| What I lift | I think… | What happened: a little, more, or a lot? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One book to my knee |  |  |
| One book to my tummy |  |  |
| Three books to my knee |  |  |
| Three books to my tummy |  |  |

For the challenge, keep the row of socks going until you run out.

**What we discovered:** Lifting something higher takes more effort, and so does lifting something heavier. That's why climbing stairs makes us tired: on every step, we lift our whole body. And a sequence always repeats in the same order.

## For grown-ups (page 24)

**The idea.** To climb, we have to lift our own body against gravity, which always pulls down: the higher we go, and the heavier the thing we lift, the more effort it takes (physicists call this work). Going down, gravity helps, but our muscles slow the body on every step so it doesn't drop all at once: that's why going down also takes strength and care.

**Sequences.** A sequence is something that follows an order: counting the steps from 1 to 6 and back down, "hand, foot, foot," Rula's colors. Spotting a pattern and guessing what comes next is one of the first building blocks of math.

**The milestone.** Many children start climbing stairs holding the railing or a hand somewhere between 2 and 3 years old: first they put both feet on each step, and later they alternate one foot per step. Going down usually comes after going up. Every child has their own pace; if you have any concerns, check with your pediatrician.

**Safety.** Put safety gates at the top and bottom of the stairs until they can go up and down safely, and don't leave toys on the steps. Always with a grown-up right beside them, in good light, barefoot or in non-slip socks.

**Things to talk about after reading:** How many steps are there in your home or at school? Shall we count them? What makes you more tired: walking or climbing? Why? Make up a sequence with claps and hops: clap, clap, hop… what comes next?
