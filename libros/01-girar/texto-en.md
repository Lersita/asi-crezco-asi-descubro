## Experiment: What Rolls and What Doesn't? (pages 22–23)

Do this with a grown-up.

**You need:** a big book or a tray resting on one or two books (a low ramp), a ball, an orange, an empty toilet paper roll, a jar lid, a small box, and an empty plastic bottle with its cap on.

1. Build the ramp with a grown-up. Keep it low.
2. Before you let go of each thing, guess: will it roll, slide, or stay still? Write it in the chart.
3. Place each thing at the top of the ramp and let go. Don't push!
4. Write down what happened.
5. Challenge: try the roll lying down and then standing up. Do the same with the lid. What changes?

| Object | I think it will… | What happened |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ball |  |  |
| Orange |  |  |
| Roll, lying down |  |  |
| Roll, standing up |  |  |
| Lid, on its edge |  |  |
| Lid, lying flat |  |  |
| Box |  |  |
| Bottle, lying down |  |  |

In each box, write or draw: rolls, slides, or stays still.

**What we found out:** round things touch the floor at a tiny point or along a thin line, and it takes only a little push to make them roll. Things with flat sides rest on a whole face: they stay still or, if the ramp is steep, they slide without rolling. The roll and the lid are round one way and flat the other way: whether they roll depends on how you set them down.

## For grown-ups (page 24)

**The idea.** Rolling means turning around an axis. A ball touches the floor at a single point, so the smallest push makes it turn. A block rests on a whole face: to tip it over, you have to lift it up over an edge. A baby does the same thing as the ball, turning around an imaginary line that runs from head to toe. Once the head and shoulder move past the base of support, the baby's own weight finishes the turn.

**The milestone.** Many babies start rolling over somewhere between 4 and 6 months. Often they roll from tummy to back first, but not always. Every baby has their own pace; if you have any concerns, check with your pediatrician.

**Safety.** Once a baby starts rolling, don't leave them alone on a bed, a changing table, or a couch. The floor is the safest place to play. For sleep, always place babies on their backs.

**Talk about it after reading**

- What other round things are in your home?
- Why are bicycle wheels round and not square?
- What were you like as a baby? Look for a photo together.

## Notas de traducción

- "Blandita" → "squishy": suena bien leído en voz alta y es la palabra que usan los chicos en inglés.
- "¡Di media vuelta!" → "I made a half turn!": mantiene la física exacta de la biblia (de boca arriba a boca abajo es media vuelta, no una vuelta entera).
- "Giro una vez…" → "I roll once, I roll twice, I roll three times": conserva la repetición para contar.
- "Para los grandes" → "For grown-ups"; la seguridad sigue la recomendación de la Academia Americana de Pediatría (dormir boca arriba).
