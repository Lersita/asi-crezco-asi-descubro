## Experiment: Float or Sink? (pages 22–23)

**You need:** a large bowl of water, an orange, a small empty plastic bottle with its cap on, a cork, a metal spoon, a small stone, and a cup.

1. Before you try, guess: which things will float and which will sink?
2. Put each thing in the water, one at a time, and watch what happens.
3. Peel the orange with a grown-up and put it back in the water. What happened this time?
4. With the cup, fill the bottle halfway and try it again. Does it float higher or lower?
5. **Challenge:** how many cups of water does it take to fill the bowl?

For the challenge, write down how many cups it took.

**What we discovered:** Some things float and some things sink. Things with lots of air inside, like the cork, the empty bottle, or the orange peel, usually float. Without its peel, the orange sinks. And when we put water in the bottle, it floats lower.

## For grown-ups (page 24)

**The idea.** Whether something floats or sinks doesn't depend on whether it's big or small, or only on whether it's light: it depends on how heavy it is for its size, compared with water. Things with lots of air inside (an inflated ball, a cork, an orange peel) weigh little for the space they take up, and the water holds them up. That's why a huge log floats and a tiny coin sinks. With our lungs full of air, our bodies float better.

**Full, empty, and volume.** Full, empty, and "half full" are the first ideas of volume: how much space there is inside something. Counting how many cups fit in a bucket is a way of measuring that space.

**The milestone.** Many children start floating with help, kicking with a kickboard, and putting their face in the water somewhere between 4 and 5 years old, especially if they take lessons. Swimming on their own comes later. Every child has their own pace; if you have any concerns, check with your pediatrician.

**Safety.** Always have a grown-up in the water and within arm's reach, even if the child can float or is wearing floaties: floaties never replace an adult. No running around the edge. Home pools need a fence and a self-latching gate. And for the experiment, use only a bowl: a young child can drown in very little water.

**Things to talk about after reading:** What things in the bathtub float? Which ones sink? Why does Rula float, if it's so big? If you fill a cup halfway, how many half-full cups make one full cup?
