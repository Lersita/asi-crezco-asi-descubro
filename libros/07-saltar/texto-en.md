## Experiment: What Bounces Highest? (pages 22–23)

**You need:** a long strip of paper, tape to stick it on the wall, a pencil, a big ball, a small ball, and a rolled-up sock.

1. Tape the paper strip to the wall, top to bottom. With a grown-up, mark a line for each of your hands, placed one above the other.
2. Before you try, guess: which one will bounce highest?
3. Let go of each thing from the height of your waist, without throwing it.
4. Watch which line it reaches after it hits the floor. Write it down.
5. **Challenge:** Stand next to the strip and jump with both feet. Which line can your hands reach?

**What we found out:** Everything that goes up comes back down: gravity always pulls down. Some things bounce a lot and others hardly at all. And nothing bounces higher than the spot where we let it go.

## For grown-ups (page 24)

**The idea.** Gravity pulls everything toward the Earth, always, even when we're in the air. When we jump, our legs push the ground and the body goes up, but gravity slows it down until it comes back down. A ball bounces because it squishes a little when it hits the ground and then springs back into shape; with each bounce it loses some of its push, so it goes up a little less each time and never higher than where it was dropped.

**The milestone.** Many children start jumping with both feet together somewhere between 2 and 3 years old: first little hops in place, then forward, and later on, down from a low step. Every child has their own pace; if you have any concerns, check with your pediatrician.

**Safety.** It's best for them to jump on the floor, on grass or a rug, and not off beds, couches, or high places. Pediatricians recommend avoiding home trampolines at this age. Always with a grown-up close by and plenty of open space around.

**Talk about it after reading:** What things in your home bounce? Which ones don't bounce at all? Can you jump and stay up in the air? Why not? How many hands tall are you? And how many hands tall is a grown-up?
